BUILD 01

Hacking the Nervous System

I built wireless devices smaller than a neuron that control brain activity — no surgery, no wires, no genetic modification.

The Problem

Current neural interfaces — deep brain stimulation, cuff electrodes, even Galvani Bioelectronics (Google × GSK) — still require invasive surgery, wires, and batteries. Non-invasive alternatives like TMS and tDCS have terrible spatial resolution. Optogenetics achieves single-cell precision but requires genetic modification. There was no technology that was both minimally invasive AND precise.

The Idea
What if you could make electronic devices so small they could be injected like drugs?

Subcellular photovoltaic devices — 10μm across, 300nm thin — that convert light into electrical signals to control individual neurons. No surgery. No wires. No batteries. Just inject them and activate with light.

Chapter 1: Building the Device

I chose organic photovoltaics — flexible, biocompatible, and scalable to subcellular dimensions. At MIT.nano's cleanroom, I fabricated these devices on 4-inch silicon wafers: layering organic semiconductors and metals through photolithography, reactive ion etching, and e-beam deposition. A full CMOS-compatible process.

The result: free-floating discs, less than 10 micrometers in diameter, 300 nanometers thick — smaller than a neuron, substrate-free, released from the wafer and suspended in solution. Injectable electronics.

Chapter 2: Probing 10-Micrometer Devices by Hand

Before testing on neurons, I needed to verify these devices generated enough power at subcellular scales. Standard probe stations can't handle 10μm devices — so I built a custom characterization setup using manual micromanipulators, probing individual devices by hand under a microscope.

The IV curves confirmed: at just 10 micrometers, these devices produce an open-circuit voltage of 280mV and can inject up to 1.5μA — more than enough to modulate a single neuron.

Chapter 3: Controlling Neurons

I released devices from the wafer, suspended them in solution, and sprinkled them onto petri dishes of live rat hippocampal neurons. Then came the patch clamp — literally poking a glass micropipette through a neuron's membrane to read its electrical activity from the inside.

With an inverted microscope, I fired near-infrared laser pulses at individual devices sitting on neurons and watched the membrane potential shift in real-time. I could depolarize or hyperpolarize neurons depending on which electrode faced the cell.

In vitro neuron–device interface — SEM at MIT.nano. Devices sit on and between neurons, connected by axonal networks.
Patched hippocampal neuron with device on top — optical microscope, ready for laser activation

I discovered something unexpected: inhibition was easier and more reliable than stimulation — and it turned out to be the bigger unsolved problem. There's no conventional way to electrically inhibit neurons because you can't inject purely negative charge with a bipolar electrode. I had stumbled onto a first.

Wireless neural inhibition — top: control (neuron fires normally), bottom: laser activates device, neuron goes silent. Same device can perform both stimulation and inhibition based on orientation.
Chapter 4: The Monopolar Breakthrough

For these devices to work as injectable electronics — floating freely inside the body — they needed to function regardless of orientation. But photovoltaic devices are inherently bipolar: they have an anode and a cathode. If the wrong side faces the neuron, you get stimulation instead of inhibition.

I dove deep into neural interfacing and invented a monopolar device architecture. Using selective parylene patterning, I insulated one electrode so that only one polarity of charge is exposed to the tissue — regardless of how the device lands.

⚡
The result: injectable devices that consistently inhibit (or stimulate) neurons no matter their orientation inside the body. This is what the US Patent covers.
First-author paper under review at Nature Communications
Chapter 5: Hacking the Peripheral Nervous System

For my master's thesis, I took this into living animals. The target: the mouse sciatic nerve — 800 micrometers in diameter — which innervates the leg muscles. I had to design everything from scratch:

  • Custom micropipettes — pulled glass to 150μm outer diameter to fit inside the nerve
  • Microsurgery protocols — using micromanipulators to isolate and access the nerve
  • Device injection — suspended devices in solution and injected into the nerve sheath
  • Verification — transected and cleared nerves to confirm device localization via confocal microscopy
  • Activation — fired a Class 4 laser at the nerve to modulate downstream leg muscles

The Benchmark: What We're Building Toward

To achieve wireless neuromodulation with our monopolar devices, I first needed to prove the concept works with optogenetics — the gold standard. I virally transfected the sciatic nerve with channelrhodopsin and activated it with blue laser light. The result: real-time muscle contraction controlled entirely by light.

This is the benchmark. The monopolar devices I invented are designed to achieve exactly this — wireless, light-driven neural control — but without any genetic modification. That's what the first-author paper under review demonstrates.

BUILD 02

Zapping Brain Tumors

I built two complete RF power systems from scratch — for $5,000 instead of $40,000 — so magnetic nanoantennas could kill glioblastoma cells for 72 hours straight without touching healthy neurons.

The Mission

Our group discovered HITMAN — a wireless bioelectric cancer therapy where magnetically actuated nanoantennas convert an externally applied RF magnetic field (≤200 kHz) into localized electric fields that selectively kill glioblastoma cells. In vitro, HITMAN decreased GBM cell viability by 52.2% (vs 10% with standard chemo), while sparing neurons and astrocytes. In animal models, it extended survival by over 50% with no systemic toxicity.

But the biology team had the nanoantennas and no way to activate them. No commercial RF system existed for this application. They needed someone to build the hardware. That was me.

What I Built: The Power Amplifier

The team needed to multiplex experiments — running in-vitro and in-vivo treatments simultaneously. A single commercial RF power amplifier costs $20,000, and we needed at least two. I designed and built two custom push-pull power amplifiers from scratch for a total cost of $5,000 — 8× cheaper than buying one commercial unit.

From circuit simulation (SPICE) through PCB design and layout to final assembly — rack-mounted amplifier boards inside a custom black-box enclosure with real-time voltage and current monitoring via green LED displays. Each amplifier drives high-current sinusoidal waveforms into inductive coils at precisely 200 kHz — continuously, for 72 hours straight. That's three full days of uninterrupted high-power RF, requiring rock-solid thermal management and component reliability.

What I Built: Taming 4,000 Volts

At resonance, the voltage across the coil system reaches approximately 4,000 volts. No single capacitor can handle that. I designed and built a matching network using an array of ~100–150 capacitors to distribute the voltage load, tuned on an LCR meter to hit the 200 kHz resonance point. Hand-wound transformer toroids complete the impedance matching chain between the amplifier and the coils.

Challenge 1: 72 Hours of Heat

This therapy doesn't pulse — it runs continuously for 72 hours. At the power levels needed to reach human safety-limit field strengths, the coils generate massive heat. In the in-vitro incubator (37°C, 5% CO₂), any temperature spike kills the cancer cells before the nanoantennas can — ruining the experiment.

My solution: active water cooling for the coils. I ran them submerged and validated that the cooling didn't detune the circuit by testing impedance at 200 kHz in water.

Testing the RF coil submerged in water — validating impedance stays tuned at 200 kHz under active cooling conditions
Challenge 2: Electronics Inside a Hostile Incubator

Cell culture incubators are warm, humid, CO₂-rich — the opposite of what electronics want. I integrated my RF system into the incubator without disrupting temperature or CO₂ levels, while delivering full-power fields to the cell plate above the coils — for three straight days.

Deploying the RF system inside the cell culture incubator — coils, cooling, and power delivery integrated without disrupting the 37°C / 5% CO₂ environment
Challenge 3: Scaling from Cells to Animals

The in-vivo system required a completely different coil geometry — a solenoid large enough for the animal to sit inside, producing 4 mT at ~200 kHz uniformly across the treatment volume. Different power requirements, different impedance matching, and deployment in an animal facility with strict safety constraints.

The result: magnetoelectric nanoparticles activated by my RF systems killed glioblastoma cells with high specificity. The biology team demonstrated tumor regression in animal models using the hardware I built from scratch.

BUILD 03

Portable NMR: Bedside Fluid Monitoring

Building portable MR hardware — from a published Nature Communications sensor deployed at Massachusetts General Hospital, to an entirely new Halbach bore magnet designed for 10× the signal.

The Problem

500,000+ dialysis patients in the US have no way to accurately measure how much fluid is in their body. Clinicians guess — and when they guess wrong, patients end up in the ER with heart failure (too much fluid) or crash during dialysis (too little). The gold standard requires threading a catheter into the heart. There is no non-invasive, quantitative bedside measurement.

Device V1: Single-Sided MR Sensor — Published in Nature Communications (2024)

We built a portable 11 kg single-sided MR sensor — a slab of 448 N52 permanent magnets that projects a 0.2 T magnetic field into the calf muscle. Using T2 relaxometry, we extract a biomarker called R2,2 that quantifies the ratio of intracellular to extracellular water in skeletal muscle. We showed that R2,2 drops during dialysis as fluid is removed, correlates with the volume removed (R² = 0.85), and distinguishes overloaded patients from healthy controls.

I updated the RF electronics and grounding for clinical robustness and led deployments at Massachusetts General Hospital with nephrologist Dr. Sagar Nigwekar for validation in dialysis patients — the first and only clinically validated portable MR diagnostic measurement.

Device V1: COMSOL simulation of the unilateral Halbach magnet array (a,b) and the constructed sensor with RF coil and Delrin casing (c,d). Published in Nature Communications.
Clinical deployment: a patient's calf resting on the single-sided MR sensor during a T₂ relaxometry measurement at the hospital.
Why Build a New Device?

The current sensor has three critical limitations:

01
SNR collapses in the clinic. The single-sided geometry has no self-shielding. Hospital EMI from infusion pumps and telemetry crushes the signal — SNR drops from >150 in the lab to as low as 20 in the clinic.
02
Tiny sweet spot. The single-sided design concentrates field in a 200 mm³ sliver at 8 mm depth. This limits signal averaging and forces 11-minute acquisitions.
03
Can’t measure the forearm. To prove R2,2 is a systemic biomarker (not gravity-dependent pooling in the legs), we need a second measurement site. The flat sensor can’t wrap around a forearm.
Device V2: What I’m Building — Cylindrical Halbach Bore Magnet

I’m designing and building an entirely new cylindrical Halbach bore magnet — a ring of 320 N52 permanent magnets arranged in a k=2 pattern so the patient slides their forearm into the center. The Halbach geometry concentrates the field inside the bore while canceling it outside, providing partial self-shielding against hospital EMI.

Building It: From Simulation to Hardware

The bore magnet uses 32 sticks of ½-inch N52 neodymium cubes (10 cubes per stick) arranged in a Halbach k=2 pattern — each stick’s magnetization is rotated by twice its angular position, concentrating the field inside the bore while canceling it outside. I 3D-printed the housing, sorted magnets by flux homogeneity, and am assembling the array by hand.

Impact: Toward Absolute Fluid Volume Status

With the new bore device measuring the forearm and the existing sensor measuring the calf, we can test the critical hypothesis: does R2,2 change proportionally at both sites during dialysis? If yes, R2,2 is validated as an absolute systemic biomarker of total body fluid volume — meaning a single bedside measurement can tell clinicians exactly how fluid-overloaded a patient is, without catheters, without guessing.

The 10× SNR improvement also unlocks faster acquisitions, higher precision, and the robustness needed for deployment in any clinical environment — the first step toward a home monitoring device for dialysis patients.

BUILD 04

Tissue Laser: Seeing Through Scattering Media

Building a holographic optical system to time-reverse scattered laser light and focus it strictly through opaque materials representing human tissue.

The Mission

Focusing light through biological tissue is a "holy grail" for noninvasive deep tissue imaging, diagnostics, and sub-cellular neural stimulation (optogenetics).

The problem is that light severely scatters when passing through tissue. The photons make it to the other side, but they emerge in completely random directions, destroying any optical focus point.

The Concept: Instead of normal focusing (left), we must reshape the wavefront (right) to counter the scattering effects of turbid media.
What I Built: Holographic Time-Reversal

I designed and aligned a complex transmissive optical setup from scratch. I used a 633 nm red laser (for maximum penetration depth), custom thick glass beam splitters, beam expanders, irises, and lenses to carefully balance interference.

Instead of a computerized Spatial Light Modulator (SLM), I used holographic media. By capturing the scattered light's complex interference pattern (the "object beam") against a clean reference beam, I recorded the exact scattering "fingerprint" of the material. By then reading the hologram with a conjugate beam, the system reconstructed the scattered wavefront backward—effectively time-reversing the light to focus it precisely into a singular point on the other side of an opaque medium.

The actual optical bench setup I built and aligned from scratch.
My schematic for the holographic recording (top) and read-out (bottom) transmissive setup.
Engineering Challenges

Challenge 1: Balancing Intensity. The object beam intensity drops massively after passing through the scattering medium. Holographic interference requires matched intensities. I bypassed typical beam splitters and used a thick glass plate at a steep angle (<45°) to utilize Fresnel reflection limits, maximizing transmission for the object beam while aggressively crushing the reference beam's strength.

Challenge 2: Multi-Reflection Noise. The holographic medium struggles with heavily distorted wavefronts. I had to reconfigure the setup with specific lenses configured at precise focal lengths to preemptively focus light onto the medium and drastically shrink wavefront distortion, while adding irises to cut out multi-reflection noise.

Impact: Focusing Through Opaque "Tissue"

I tested the system on a plastic lid (weak scattering) and a 1mm ABS plastic plate, which is highly scattering, essentially opaque, and has optical properties comparable to actual human tissue.

The Result: The customized holographic read-out successfully time-reversed the scattered wavefront, allowing me to achieve a sharp, focused dot on the opposite side of both materials—visually proving the capability to reconstruct and focus scattered waveforms strictly through tissue proxies.

Micro Devices: Cleanroom Side Builds

Beyond my primary research focus, I design, engineer, and cleanroom-fabricate customized micro-scale devices for specialized biomedical experiments when off-the-shelf tools fall short. These physical layouts must be meticulously translated from CAD (L-Edit) to physical hardware through precise nanofabrication.

Blood-Brain Barrier Organ-on-Chip

Designed and fabricated dual-chamber microfluidic chips across an entire silicon wafer. Featuring optimized flow channels, these custom chips allow for the co-culture of relevant cell types to model the BBB, enabling researchers to evaluate barrier integrity and execute complex drug transport studies.

High-Density Neural MEAs

Designed custom Microelectrode Arrays (MEAs) with intricate radial and parallel trace layouts converging on precision microelectrodes. These platforms were built specifically for high-fidelity extracellular electrophysiology recordings and precise cellular stimulation of neuronal networks.
