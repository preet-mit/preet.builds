BUILD 01

Hacking the Nervous System

I built wireless silicon discs — 10 micrometers across, thinner than a sheet of cling wrap — that silence or fire individual neurons when hit with a pulse of infrared light. No skull drills, no trailing wires, no viral injections.

The Problem

Current neural interfaces — deep brain stimulation, cuff electrodes, even Galvani Bioelectronics (Google × GSK) — still need a surgeon to drill through skull bone, thread lead wires into brain tissue, and screw a battery pack under the collarbone. Non-invasive alternatives like TMS fire magnetic coils the size of a tennis racket — activating millions of neurons at once with no spatial precision. Optogenetics hits single cells, but only after a virus rewrites the neuron's DNA. There was no technology that was both minimally invasive AND precise.

The Idea
What if you could make electronic devices so small they could be injected like drugs?

Tiny solar cells — 10μm across, 300nm thin — small enough to sit on a single neuron like a coin on a dinner plate. Hit them with a pulse of near-infrared laser light, and they pump current directly into the cell beneath them. No scalpel. No trailing wires. No battery pack. Just draw them into a syringe and inject.

Chapter 1: Building the Device

I gowned up in MIT.nano's Class-100 cleanroom and built these on 4-inch silicon wafers — spinning organic semiconductor layers at 3000 RPM, patterning them with UV photolithography, etching trenches with reactive ion plasma, and depositing metal contacts atom-by-atom in an e-beam evaporator. Twenty-three steps, each one capable of killing the entire wafer.

The result: free-floating discs, less than 10 micrometers in diameter, 300 nanometers thick. I dissolved the sacrificial layer and watched thousands of devices lift off the wafer and drift into solution — a vial of transparent liquid with invisible electronics suspended inside. Injectable electronics.

Chapter 2: Probing 10-Micrometer Devices by Hand

Before touching a neuron, I needed to prove these specks of silicon could actually push current. Standard probe stations can't land tips on a 10μm target — so I built a custom rig: two tungsten needle probes on manual micromanipulators, a microscope objective overhead, and my hands on the dials, nudging probe tips onto individual devices one at a time.

The IV curves on the screen confirmed it: at just 10 micrometers, each device kicks out 280 mV open-circuit and can push 1.5 μA into a load — more than enough current to shift a neuron's membrane potential.

Chapter 3: Controlling Neurons

I pipetted the device suspension over petri dishes of live rat hippocampal neurons — thousands of 10μm discs settling like dust onto a carpet of axons and dendrites. Then the patch clamp: I pulled a borosilicate glass capillary to a 1μm tip, pressed it against a neuron's membrane until I felt the seal resistance spike on the amplifier, and ruptured through to read the cell's voltage from the inside.

Through the inverted microscope, I aimed near-infrared laser pulses at a single device perched on a neuron and watched the green voltage trace on my monitor swing in real-time. Flip the device one way: the neuron fires. Flip it the other way: the neuron goes silent.

In vitro neuron–device interface — SEM at MIT.nano. Devices sit on and between neurons, connected by axonal networks.
Patched hippocampal neuron with device on top — optical microscope, ready for laser activation

I noticed something unexpected on the oscilloscope: silencing neurons was easier and more repeatable than firing them — and that turned out to be the bigger unsolved problem in the field. Every existing electrode has two poles; you can't push purely negative charge into a cell without the positive pole pulling it back. I had stumbled onto a first: reliable wireless neural inhibition, at subcellular resolution.

Wireless neural inhibition — top: control (neuron fires normally), bottom: laser activates device, neuron goes silent. Same device can perform both stimulation and inhibition based on orientation.
Chapter 4: The Monopolar Breakthrough

For these devices to work once injected — tumbling freely through tissue — they need to do the same thing no matter which face lands on the neuron. But a photovoltaic cell has a top (anode) and a bottom (cathode). Land upside-down, and you stimulate when you meant to silence.

I invented a monopolar architecture: I deposited a 500nm coat of parylene-C over one electrode, sealing it off from the tissue like shrink-wrap over a contact pad. Now only one polarity of charge touches the neuron — no matter how the disc tumbles and settles.

The result: injectable devices that consistently inhibit (or stimulate) neurons no matter their orientation inside the body. This is what the US Patent covers.

Chapter 5: Hacking the Peripheral Nervous System

For my master's thesis, I moved from petri dishes to living mice. The target: the sciatic nerve — a pale cord 800 micrometers wide, running from the spine down to the leg muscles. Everything had to be built from scratch:

  • Custom micropipettes — I heated borosilicate capillaries in a puller and drew them to a 150μm tip, thin enough to slide inside the nerve sheath
  • Microsurgery — under a stereo microscope, I peeled back muscle and fascia with forceps to expose the nerve, then clamped it on a micromanipulator stage
  • Injection — I loaded device suspension into the glass pipette and pressure-injected directly into the nerve
  • Verification — I sliced the nerve, cleared it with chemical reagents, and imaged the devices glowing inside under a confocal microscope
  • Activation — I aimed a Class 4 near-infrared laser beam at the nerve and watched the leg muscles downstream twitch on command

The Benchmark: What We're Building Toward

Before deploying my devices in vivo, I needed a benchmark. I injected a virus carrying channelrhodopsin into the sciatic nerve, waited two weeks for the neurons to express the light-sensitive protein, then aimed a blue laser fiber at the nerve. The mouse's leg kicked. Every pulse of blue light — a kick. Light off — the leg relaxed.

That's the benchmark. My monopolar devices are engineered to replicate exactly that muscle twitch — but without the virus, without the two-week wait, without rewriting the neuron's DNA. Just inject the silicon discs and fire the infrared laser. That's what my first-author paper under review demonstrates.

BUILD 02

Zapping Brain Tumors

I soldered, wound, and rack-mounted two complete RF power systems from scratch — for $5,000 instead of $40,000 — to drive magnetic nanoantennas that kill glioblastoma cells over 72 continuous hours without harming the neurons next door.

The Mission

Our group discovered HITMAN — a therapy where magnetoelectric nanoparticles, each smaller than a bacterium, are delivered into tumor tissue and then hit with an external AC magnetic field at 200 kHz. The oscillating field converts to localized electric fields at the nanoparticle surface — fields strong enough to punch holes in glioblastoma cell membranes — killing 52.2% of tumor cells (vs 10% with standard chemo) while the neurons and astrocytes sitting millimeters away survive unharmed. In mice, tumors shrank and survival stretched past 50% longer than controls, with no weight loss or organ damage.

But the biology team had vials of nanoparticles and no hardware to activate them. No commercial RF amplifier runs continuously at 200 kHz for three days. They needed someone to build the system from a blank workbench. That was me.

What I Built: The Power Amplifier

The lab needed two systems running simultaneously — one for cell plates in the incubator, one for live animals. A single commercial RF amplifier costs $20,000. I built two from scratch for $5,000 total — 8× cheaper than buying one off the shelf.

I started in SPICE, simulating the push-pull topology, then laid out the PCB in KiCad, etched it, populated it with MOSFETs and gate drivers, and bolted the boards into a rack-mounted black steel enclosure. Green LED displays on the front panel show real-time voltage and current. Each amplifier shoves high-current sine waves into copper coils at exactly 200 kHz — continuously, for 72 hours. That's three days without a hiccup, with heat sinks clamped to aluminum plates and fans blowing across the MOSFETs around the clock.

What I Built: Taming 4,000 Volts

At resonance, the voltage across the coil swings to 4,000 volts — enough to arc across an air gap. No single capacitor survives that. I soldered together banks of 100–150 ceramic capacitors in series-parallel ladders to split the voltage across dozens of rated bodies, then tuned each bank on an LCR meter until the impedance dipped precisely at 200 kHz. I wound the matching transformers myself — ferrite toroid cores wrapped with magnet wire, turn by turn, checking inductance after every layer.

Challenge 1: 72 Hours of Heat

This therapy doesn't pulse — it runs nonstop for 72 hours. At full power the copper coils get hot enough to burn skin. Inside the incubator — held at 37°C with 5% CO₂ — even a 2°C spike cooks the cancer cells before the nanoantennas get a chance, and the experiment is dead.

My fix: I submerged the coils in a water bath with circulating coolant lines. Then I clipped the LCR meter leads back on, dunked the whole assembly, and confirmed the resonance peak hadn't shifted — still 200 kHz, even underwater.

Testing the RF coil submerged in water — validating impedance stays tuned at 200 kHz under active cooling conditions
Challenge 2: Electronics Inside a Hostile Incubator

A cell incubator is 37°C, dripping with condensation, and filled with 5% CO₂ — a sauna that corrodes solder joints and fogs optics. I threaded the coils and coolant lines through the incubator's cable port, sealed it with silicone gaskets, and ran the system at full power for three straight days without the temperature gauge or CO₂ sensor budging.

Deploying the RF system inside the cell culture incubator — coils, cooling, and power delivery integrated without disrupting the 37°C / 5% CO₂ environment
Challenge 3: Scaling from Cells to Animals

The animal system needed a completely different coil — a solenoid wide enough for a mouse to sit inside a plastic tube at its center, producing a uniform 4 mT field at 200 kHz across the entire tumor volume. New coil dimensions meant re-winding the matching transformers, re-tuning the capacitor banks, and hauling the whole rack on a cart to the animal facility across campus.

The result: nanoantennas activated by my RF hardware shredded glioblastoma membranes while leaving healthy brain tissue intact. Tumors shrank on MRI. Mice in the treatment group were still running on their wheels weeks after controls had been euthanized.

BUILD 03

Portable NMR: Bedside Fluid Monitoring

Building portable MR hardware — from a published Nature Communications sensor deployed at Massachusetts General Hospital, to an entirely new Halbach bore magnet designed for 10× the signal.

The Problem

Three times a week, 500,000+ Americans sit in a dialysis chair while a machine pulls fluid from their blood. The nurse picks a target weight — and guesses. Guess too little: the patient's lungs fill with fluid overnight, and an ambulance brings them to the ER. Guess too much: their blood pressure crashes mid-session, and they slump in the chair. The only accurate measurement requires threading a Swan-Ganz catheter through a neck vein into the heart. There is no non-invasive bedside number.

Device V1: Single-Sided MR Sensor — Published in Nature Communications (2024)

The previous student built an 11-kilogram magnet slab — 448 N52 neodymium cubes epoxied into a Delrin housing — that projects a 0.2 T magnetic field eight millimeters into a patient's calf muscle. Using T2 relaxometry, we extract a biomarker called R2,2: essentially, the ratio of water trapped inside muscle cells to the water sloshing between them. The lab showed that R2,2 drops as the dialysis machine pulls fluid out, tracks the volume removed (R² = 0.85), and cleanly separates fluid-overloaded patients from healthy controls.

When I joined the project, I re-soldered the RF front-end, added copper shielding tape around the coil housing, and re-grounded the preamp to kill the 60 Hz noise that the hospital's infusion pumps were injecting. Then I wheeled the sensor on a cart into the dialysis unit at Massachusetts General Hospital and ran measurements on patients mid-treatment with nephrologist Dr. Sagar Nigwekar — the first and only clinically validated portable MR diagnostic measurement.

Device V1: COMSOL simulation of the unilateral Halbach magnet array (a,b) and the constructed sensor with RF coil and Delrin casing (c,d). Published in Nature Communications.
Clinical deployment: a patient's calf resting on the single-sided MR sensor during a T₂ relaxometry measurement at the hospital.
Why Build a New Device?

The current sensor has three critical limitations:

01
SNR collapses in the clinic. The single-sided geometry has no self-shielding. Hospital EMI from infusion pumps and telemetry crushes the signal — SNR drops from >150 in the lab to as low as 20 in the clinic.
02
Tiny sweet spot. The single-sided design concentrates field in a 200 mm³ sliver at 8 mm depth. This limits signal averaging and forces 11-minute acquisitions.
03
Can’t measure the forearm. To prove R2,2 is a systemic biomarker (not gravity-dependent pooling in the legs), we need a second measurement site. The flat sensor can’t wrap around a forearm.
Device V2: What I’m Building — Cylindrical Halbach Bore Magnet

I’m building an entirely new magnet from scratch: a cylindrical Halbach bore — 320 N52 neodymium cubes pressed into a 3D-printed ring, each one's north pole rotated at twice its angular position (the k=2 rule). The patient slides their forearm into the open bore like sliding a hand into a coffee can. The Halbach pattern crushes the field to near-zero outside the ring while amplifying it inside — a built-in shield against the 60 Hz hum of hospital infusion pumps and telemetry monitors.

Building It: From Simulation to Hardware

32 sticks, each made of 10 half-inch N52 neodymium cubes snapped end-to-end — strong enough to pinch skin if you're careless. I printed the housing on an FDM printer in PLA, with 32 angled slots cut to sub-degree tolerance. Before loading, I held every cube against a gaussmeter probe and binned them by flux strength, discarding outliers. Assembly is by hand — wearing leather gloves, wedging each stick into its slot against the fierce repulsion of its neighbors, one at a time.

Impact: Toward Absolute Fluid Volume Status

Picture a dialysis session: one magnet slab under the patient's calf, the new bore around their forearm. Both sensors pinging the muscle every three minutes. If R2,2 drops at the same rate in both limbs as the machine pulls fluid, then R2,2 isn't just measuring leg swelling — it's reading the whole body. One number, from one arm, replaces the Swan-Ganz catheter in the neck.

The 10× SNR boost also means the scan finishes in under three minutes instead of eleven, and the signal stays clean even with infusion pumps beeping two feet away — robust enough that eventually a patient could strap the bore on at home, take a reading before breakfast, and text the number to their nephrologist. A bathroom-scale for body fluid.

BUILD 04

Tissue Laser: Seeing Through Scattering Media

Building a holographic optical system to time-reverse scattered laser light and focus it strictly through opaque materials representing human tissue.

The Mission

Shine a laser pointer at your fingertip: you see a red glow, but no dot. The photons made it through, but the collagen fibers, cell membranes, and fat droplets inside your skin scrambled them into a diffuse blur.

If you could un-scramble that light — focus it back to a sharp point on the far side of living tissue — you'd unlock noninvasive deep-tissue imaging, pinpoint drug activation, and single-cell optogenetics without ever cutting skin. That's the challenge.

The Concept: Instead of normal focusing (left), we must reshape the wavefront (right) to counter the scattering effects of turbid media.
What I Built: Holographic Time-Reversal

I bolted a 633 nm helium-neon laser to an optical breadboard and spent two weeks aligning the beam path by hand — adjusting thick glass beam splitters on micrometer stages, threading the beam through brass irises, and walking it through a pair of plano-convex lenses to expand it to a clean, collimated disk of red light.

Instead of a $15,000 computerized Spatial Light Modulator, I used a sheet of holographic film. I split the laser into two arms: one passed through the scattering sample (the scrambled "object beam"), the other stayed clean (the "reference beam"). Where the two met on the film, their interference fringes burned a permanent record of the sample's scattering fingerprint. To read it back, I flipped the reference beam's direction — and the hologram reconstructed the scattered wavefront in reverse, collapsing the blur back into a single bright dot on the far side of the opaque plate.

The actual optical bench setup I built and aligned from scratch.
My schematic for the holographic recording (top) and read-out (bottom) transmissive setup.
Engineering Challenges

Challenge 1: Lopsided beams. After punching through the scattering plate, the object beam was a dim, milky smear — 100× weaker than the clean reference beam. Holographic fringes need matched brightness. I swapped the standard 50/50 beam splitter for a thick glass plate tilted past 45° — at that steep angle, Fresnel reflection skims off barely 4% for the reference arm while letting 96% of the power slam through to the object arm. Brute-force intensity balancing with a slab of glass.

Challenge 2: Ghost fringes. Stray reflections bouncing inside the glass plates printed false fringes on the holographic film — noise that washed out the real signal. I clamped two brass irises in the beam path to clip the outer rays, then added a converging lens one focal length before the film to shrink the scrambled wavefront down to a tight patch, concentrating the signal and starving the ghosts.

Impact: Focusing Through Opaque "Tissue"

I tested on two samples: a thin plastic lid (weak scattering — like looking through frosted glass) and a 1 mm ABS plate — chalky white, fully opaque, with scattering properties that mimic a slab of human dermis.

The result: I slid the holographic plate into its mount, flipped the conjugate beam on, and on the white card behind the ABS plate, a single red dot snapped into focus — sharp and round, no bigger than a pinhead. Light, focused through a wall it couldn't see through.

BUILD 05

Micro Devices: Cleanroom Side Builds

When a collaborator's experiment needs a chip that doesn't exist in any catalog, I draw it in L-Edit, book time in the cleanroom, and fabricate it on a silicon wafer — translating CAD polygons into physical metal traces, etched channels, and bonding pads I can hold on a fingertip.

Blood-Brain Barrier Organ-on-Chip

I etched dual-chamber microfluidic chips into a 4-inch silicon wafer — nine identical chips per wafer, each with a serpentine flow channel narrow enough to trap a single layer of endothelial cells on one side and astrocytes on the other. Researchers pump fluorescent dye through one chamber and watch how much leaks across the cell barrier, testing whether a new drug opens or closes the blood-brain barrier gate.

High-Density Neural MEAs

I drew radial gold traces — thinner than a human hair — that fan out from a central ring of 60 microelectrodes, each one a 30μm circle of exposed platinum. Neurons grow directly on top of these pads; when they fire, the electrodes pick up the 50μV spike and route it down the trace to a bonding pad at the chip's edge, where a ribbon cable carries the signal to a recording amplifier.
